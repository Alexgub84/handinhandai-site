# Hebrew voice research: writing like a person, not a generator

Research pass for Hand in Hand AI's Hebrew copy (site, case studies, SEO content).
Goal: a checklist of concrete tells to strip out, typography rules to get right,
and a controlled dose of humour that reads as Israeli-professional, not a bit.

Every item is tagged:
- **[sourced]** — backed by a cited page (Academy of the Hebrew Language ruling,
  a named Hebrew-writing/editing guide, a linguistics reference).
- **[judgement]** — my own assessment from reading the sourced material and
  general Hebrew usage; no single citation pins it down. Marked honestly rather
  than dressed up as a ruling.

Sources are linked inline at first use and collected at the bottom.

---

## 1. The tells — what makes Hebrew read as machine-written

30 items. Each: the phrase, why it reads as generated, what a person writes instead.

### Opening formulas

1. **"בעולם של היום"** (in today's world) — a stock throat-clearing opener that
   adds zero information before the sentence even starts. Flagged explicitly as
   an AI signature by a Hebrew anti-AI-writing guide. **[sourced]** ([practice-ai.co.il](https://practice-ai.co.il/skills/avoid-ai-writing))
   → Person writes: starts with the actual claim. "תוכנה שרואה פיגומים לפני שהם נופלים."
2. **"בעידן הדיגיטלי"** (in the digital age) — same move, dressed as a
   time-period claim nobody needed stated. **[judgement]**, by extension of #1.
   → Cut it; name the specific thing that changed.
3. **"בשוק התחרותי של היום"** (in today's competitive market) — stacks two
   empty-time openers into one. **[judgement]**.
   → Name the actual competitor or constraint, or drop the clause.
4. **"בעידן של בינה מלאכותית"** (in the age of AI) — meta version of #2, common
   in exactly the kind of copy this site writes. **[judgement]**.
   → State what the AI actually does in this sentence, skip the era-naming.

### Filler transitions / hedge phrases

5. **"חשוב לציין ש..."** (it's important to note that) — a transition that
   announces importance instead of earning it. Named as an AI tell. **[sourced]** ([practice-ai.co.il](https://practice-ai.co.il/skills/avoid-ai-writing))
   → Just state the fact; if it's important, the reader will notice.
6. **"ראוי להדגיש"** (it is worth emphasizing) — same family, more formal
   register than anyone speaks in. **[judgement]**.
   → Drop, or "וזה מה שחשוב:" if a transition is truly needed.
7. **"יש לציין כי"** (it should be noted that) — passive, agentless, classic
   translated-bureaucratic register. **[judgement]**.
   → Say who is noting what, in active voice.
8. **"ללא ספק"** (without a doubt) — asserts certainty instead of
   demonstrating it; flagged as an AI tell. **[sourced]** ([practice-ai.co.il](https://practice-ai.co.il/skills/avoid-ai-writing))
   → Cut it. If the claim is solid, the evidence carries it.
9. **"יתרה מכך"** (moreover) — stiff written-register connector that doesn't
   occur in speech; appears disproportionately in generated Hebrew paragraphs.
   **[judgement]**, drawn from pattern discussion in a Hebrew AI-writing guide
   ([thecyborg.co.il](https://thecyborg.co.il/skills/guides/hebrew-that-comes-out-professional/)).
   → "וגם," or just start the next sentence.
10. **"בסופו של יום"** (at the end of the day) — overused connector/closer that
    signals a wrap-up is coming before anything is actually concluded.
    **[judgement]**.
    → State the conclusion directly.
11. **"לסיכום"** (in summary) opening a final paragraph that restates the piece —
    flagged directly as an AI pattern. **[sourced]** ([practice-ai.co.il](https://practice-ai.co.il/skills/avoid-ai-writing))
    → End on the last concrete point or the call to action; no recap paragraph.

### Marketing adjective stacks

12. **"פתרונות מתקדמים"** (advanced solutions) — "פתרון" (solution) plus any
    adjective is near-meaningless; says nothing about what the thing does.
    **[judgement]**.
    → Name the actual function: "מערכת שמזהה סדקים בתמונה."
13. **"חדשניים ומובילים"** (innovative and leading) — paired adjectives that
    claim status rather than show it; the "X ו-Y" adjective pair is itself a
    pattern (see also #22, symmetric lists). **[judgement]**.
    → Cut both; if genuinely first in a category, state the specific fact
    ("הראשונה בישראל ש...").
14. **"איכותי ומקצועי"** (quality and professional) — generic double
    self-praise with no checkable content. **[judgement]**.
    → Describe one concrete thing that makes it good.
15. **"מהפכני"** (revolutionary) — inflated claim a reader discounts on sight.
    **[judgement]**.
    → Let the result speak; state the before/after.
16. **"מקצה לקצה"** (end-to-end) as a floating adjective rather than describing
    a real pipeline — a calque of English "end-to-end solution" bolted onto
    anything. **[judgement]**.
    → Only use it when you can actually name the two ends.

### Over-formal register where spoken Hebrew uses a simpler word

17. **"הרינו מתכבדים"** (we hereby have the honor) — archaic business register;
    a Hebrew copywriting guide names this explicitly as what to avoid, contrasted
    with "write as you speak." **[sourced]** ([danielzrihen.co.il](https://danielzrihen.co.il/how-to-write-better/))
    → "אנחנו," plain and done.
18. **"לרכוש"** (to purchase, elevated register) where spoken Hebrew says
    **"לקנות"** (to buy). **[judgement]**.
19. **"להסיר"** (to remove, formal/legal register) where spoken Hebrew says
    **"למחוק"** or **"להוריד"** depending on context. **[judgement]**.
20. **"ליישם"** (to implement/apply, bureaucratic) where a plain verb like
    **"לעשות"** or a specific verb names the action better. **[judgement]**.
21. **"אנו"** (we, formal/literary) instead of the spoken **"אנחנו"** — a single
    letter swap that is nonetheless a strong register signal; formal Hebrew
    business writing defaults to "אנו," a human writer who wants to sound
    like a person uses "אנחנו." **[judgement]**.

### Calques from English word order / English idiom translated literally

22. **"לנצל את ההזדמנות"** (to exploit/leverage the opportunity), **"לגלות את
    הפוטנציאל"** (discover the potential), **"לפתוח את היכולות שלך"** (unlock
    your capabilities) — direct translations of English marketing verbs
    (unleash / discover / unlock) that no Israeli actually says. Named
    explicitly as translated-English patterns to fix. **[sourced]** (search
    synthesis citing common AI-Hebrew guidance, consistent with
    [danielzrihen.co.il](https://danielzrihen.co.il/how-to-write-better/) and
    [thecyborg.co.il](https://thecyborg.co.il/skills/guides/hebrew-that-comes-out-professional/))
    → Say what the person can now do, literally: "אתה רואה את זה לפני שזה קורה."
23. Fronting a long subordinate clause before the verb because that is where
    English puts it ("Because the system tracks every change in real time, you
    always know..." → "מכיוון שהמערכת עוקבת אחרי כל שינוי בזמן אמת, אתה יודע
    תמיד...") — grammatical in Hebrew but unnatural ordering; spoken Hebrew
    puts the main clause first. **[judgement]**.
    → "אתה יודע תמיד מה קרה, כי המערכת עוקבת אחרי כל שינוי בזמן אמת."
24. **"אנחנו לא נשכנע אותך, הנתונים ישכנעו אותך"**-style false-modesty framing
    translated from English copywriting voice — coy and self-referential in a
    way Hebrew business prose doesn't naturally produce. **[judgement]**.
    → Show the number. Don't narrate the act of persuading.

### Overuse of the construct state (סמיכות)

25. Stacking construct chains instead of a prepositional phrase — e.g.
    **"מערכת ניהול פרויקטי בניה"** (construction-project management system)
    where **"מערכת לניהול פרויקטים בבנייה"** reads more naturally and avoids
    three nouns welded together. The construct state (סמיכות) is a real and
    productive Hebrew structure, but AI output leans on it reflexively for
    any noun phrase, producing the same dense multi-word chains repeatedly.
    **[judgement]**, built on the general linguistic description of סמיכות as
    a head-complement structure used to form compound nouns.
    → Break long chains with "של" or a preposition once there are three nouns
    or more.
26. **"חוסר הוודאות"** / **"חוסר השקיפות"** as a reflex nominal pair instead of
    a plain sentence ("אי אפשר לדעת" / "אי אפשר לראות מה קורה"). **[judgement]**.

### "אשר" where a plain relative clause belongs

27. **"אשר"** (which/that, Biblical/formal register) used as the default
    relative pronoun instead of the spoken **"ש-"**. Historically אשר is the
    earlier form and ש- the Rabbinic-Hebrew successor that became the ordinary
    spoken form; using אשר today signals elevated, literary register
    (מליצית). **[sourced]** (historical development:
    [leshoniada.co.il](https://leshoniada.co.il/2025/09/07/%D7%90%D7%A9%D7%A8-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A9-%D7%9E%D7%94-%D7%94%D7%91%D7%93%D7%9C/);
    the "elevated/formal effect" judgement is mine)
    → "המערכת ש-" not "המערכת אשר."

### Nominalisation instead of verbs

28. **"ביצוע אימות של הנתונים"** (the performance of verification of the data)
    instead of **"לאמת את הנתונים"** (to verify the data) — turning a verb into
    an abstract action-noun ("שם פעולה") is a known Hebrew nominalisation
    pattern, and generated text reaches for it constantly because it sounds
    more formal/official. **[sourced]** (nominalisation as a described Hebrew
    morphological process, via action-noun derivation from verb patterns —
    search synthesis of linguistic sources) + **[judgement]** on the AI-overuse
    claim specifically.
    → Use the verb: "המערכת מאמתת את הנתונים."
29. **"התבצעה עדכון"** / **"נעשה תהליך של בדיקה"** — wrapping a simple verb in
    a noun plus a weak supporting verb ("נעשה תהליך של..."). **[judgement]**.
    → "עדכנו," "בדקנו." One verb, not a noun plus a verb carrying it.

### Symmetric three-item lists

30. The reflex triad — almost any generated paragraph resolves its claim into
    exactly three parallel items ("מהיר, פשוט ואמין" / "חוסך זמן, כסף ומאמץ"),
    each matched in length and grammatical form. Real human copy produces lists
    of two, four, or one, driven by what's actually true, not by a rule of
    three. **[judgement]**.
    → Count what's actually there. If it's two things, write two.

### The closing summary paragraph that adds nothing

31. A final paragraph beginning **"לסיכום"** or **"בשורה התחתונה"** that
    restates the piece's points in miniature instead of ending on the last
    real point or a call to action. Explicitly named as a generated-text
    pattern. **[sourced]** ([practice-ai.co.il](https://practice-ai.co.il/skills/avoid-ai-writing))
    → Delete the paragraph. End on the strongest concrete claim.

### Additional generic-marketing tells

32. **"אנחנו החברה מספר 1"** (we're company #1) / unverifiable superiority
    claims — named directly as the kind of self-promotion that makes readers
    go check the competitor instead. **[sourced]** ([danielzrihen.co.il](https://danielzrihen.co.il/how-to-write-better/))
    → Only claim rank with a source attached, or don't claim it.
33. Long sentences that can't be read in one breath without losing the thread —
    a named marker of weak/generated Hebrew prose; the fix given is explicit:
    one idea per paragraph, short sentences. **[sourced]** ([danielzrihen.co.il](https://danielzrihen.co.il/how-to-write-better/))
    → Split at the first "ו-" that is doing the job of a period.
34. Prompt-language leakage: Hebrew output that was generated from an
    English-language prompt reads as translated even when the grammar is
    correct, because the underlying structure was composed in English and
    then rendered into Hebrew words. Prompting in Hebrew produces measurably
    more natural flow. **[sourced]** (synthesis of Hebrew AI-writing guidance
    on prompt language and output naturalness, e.g.
    [thecyborg.co.il](https://thecyborg.co.il/skills/guides/hebrew-that-comes-out-professional/))
    → When writing or revising, think the sentence in Hebrew first; don't
    translate an English draft.

## 2. The mechanics that betray a machine

Typography and punctuation. Academy of the Hebrew Language rulings cited
where they exist; general usage noted otherwise.

1. **Geresh (גרש, ׳) vs. gershayim (גרשיים, ״) vs. straight quotes.** The
   geresh marks a single-letter abbreviation and sits after the last letter
   written (e.g. **מ'** for מטר). The gershayim marks a multi-letter acronym
   and sits before the last letter of the acronym (e.g. **צה"ל**, **רמטכ"ל**,
   **ח"כ**, and this holds in inflected forms: **ח"כים**, **רס"ריות**,
   **צה"לי**). Generated and machine-translated text routinely uses a plain
   straight apostrophe `'` or quotation mark `"` (the ASCII characters) instead
   of the correct typographic geresh/gershayim glyphs, or places the mark after
   the last letter of an acronym instead of before it. **[sourced]**
   ([hebrew-academy.org.il topic/hahlatot/punctuation](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/hahlatot/punctuation/);
   acronym placement confirmed via
   [Wikipedia גרשיים](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%92%D7%A8%D7%A9%D7%99%D7%99%D7%9D)
   and [safa-ivrit.org](https://www.safa-ivrit.org/punctuation/double-geresh.php))
2. **Quotation marks (מירכאות) for speech and titles.** Hebrew encloses direct
   speech, quotations, and the names of works (a book, article, song, film) or
   the proper name of an institution in מירכאות — the same double-quote glyph
   as gershayim but used for a different grammatical job (quoting vs.
   abbreviating). Machine output often can't tell the two uses apart
   typographically because it renders both as a straight `"`. **[sourced]**
   ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/hahlatot/punctuation/))
3. **Maqaf (מקף) vs. dash (קו מפריד) — connect vs. separate.** These are two
   different marks with opposite jobs and opposite typography. The maqaf is
   short, sits at the top of the line, and has **no space** before or after it
   — it joins ("המקף קצר, אין רווח לפניו ואחריו, ומקומו המקובל בדפוסים בגובה
   השורה"). The dash is longer, centred vertically, and **does** take a space
   on each side — it separates. Generated Hebrew text frequently uses one glyph
   (usually a plain hyphen `-`) for both jobs, or puts spaces around a maqaf.
   **[sourced]** ([hebrew-academy.org.il, "על מקף וקו מפריד"](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%9E%D7%A7%D7%A3-%D7%95%D7%A7%D7%95-%D7%9E%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%93/))
4. **When to use a maqaf.** Standard cases: prefixed constructions (**אי־ודאות**,
   **דו־משמעות**), joining a Hebrew letter/word to a digit or Latin fragment
   (**ה־15**, **ה־CEO**), and some two-part proper names (**תל־אביב**,
   **בן־יהודה**). Some writers extend it to ordinary compounds (**כתבי־יד**,
   **יום־יום**); this is a style choice, not a hard rule. **[sourced]**
   ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%9E%D7%A7%D7%A3-%D7%95%D7%A7%D7%95-%D7%9E%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%93/))
5. **Latin technical terms inside an RTL sentence.** In practice, Hebrew tech
   and business writing attaches the definite article with a maqaf directly to
   the Latin term — **ה-API**, **ה-CEO**, **ה-KPI של Q3** — keeping the Latin
   span itself untouched (no Hebrew letters mixed inside the Latin word).
   **[judgement]** on the general convention (observed in real technical
   Hebrew usage, e.g. official Hebrew developer documentation using "ה-API");
   no single Academy ruling covers code-switched tech terms specifically.
   Punctuation that belongs to the Hebrew sentence (comma, period, the
   gershayim of a following Hebrew acronym) stays outside the Latin span;
   don't let bidi reordering pull a Hebrew comma to the wrong side of the
   Latin word — proofread the rendered line, not just the source text.
   **[judgement]**.
6. **Numbers in RTL text.** Digits themselves are always typed left-to-right
   (1234, not reversed) even inside an RTL sentence; the Unicode bidi algorithm
   handles this automatically in any correctly-encoded Hebrew text, so a
   reversed or broken number is a strong tell of broken encoding/copy-paste,
   not a style choice. Short numbers (0–10, round tens/hundreds) are
   conventionally spelled out in words; long or frequent/data-style numbers are
   written as digits. Decimal point uses a period (0.5), not a comma; numbers
   of five digits or more may use a thousands comma. **[sourced]**
   ([hebrew-academy.org.il, "כתיבת מספרים"](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%99%D7%91%D7%AA-%D7%9E%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D/))
7. **Percent sign.** Written immediately after the digit, no space:
   **5%**. **[sourced]** ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%99%D7%91%D7%AA-%D7%9E%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D/))
8. **Shekel sign (₪).** The Academy's recommendation is to place the symbol to
   the *left* of the number with no space — but because ₪ is not marked in
   Unicode's bidi tables as attaching the way currency symbols do in other
   RTL conventions, typing ₪ after the number is what actually renders
   correctly and consistently across apps today; typing it "correctly" per the
   Academy's left-placement can render visually wrong depending on the
   renderer. In practice, **₪100** (symbol first, no space) is both the
   common convention and the version likeliest to render correctly.
   **[sourced]**, with the rendering caveat **[judgement]**
   ([he.wikipedia.org/שקל_חדש](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%A9%D7%A7%D7%9C_%D7%97%D7%93%D7%A9);
   Academy numbers page above)
9. **Dates.** The Academy's preferred written-out form is **day במונת year**
   style, e.g. **31 בינואר 2026**; all-numeric dates (31.1.2026 or
   31/1/2026) are acceptable; a date *range* is written left-to-right in the
   order start–end even inside the RTL sentence (**1.1.2026–31.12.2026**).
   **[sourced]** ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%99%D7%91%D7%AA-%D7%9E%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D/))
10. **Time.** Colon format is the modern preferred form: **12:00**, not
    **12.00** or **1200**. **[sourced]** ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%99%D7%91%D7%AA-%D7%9E%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D/))
11. **Spacing around parentheses in RTL.** Parentheses behave like any other
    paired punctuation — no space between the parenthesis and the text it
    encloses, a normal word-space before the opening and after the closing
    mark. Bidi rendering sometimes visually "flips" which glyph looks like an
    open vs. close paren on screen, but the logical Unicode characters and the
    no-inner-space rule don't change; a generated or badly copy-pasted string
    sometimes carries a stray space just inside the parenthesis, which is the
    actual, checkable tell. **[judgement]**.
12. **Space rule shared by maqaf and gershayim-inside-a-word.** The Academy's
    punctuation rules explicitly list two marks that take *no* surrounding
    space at all: the maqaf joining words, and gershayim sitting inside a word
    (as opposed to a quotation's gershayim at a phrase boundary). A generated
    text that adds a space before/after either is detectably wrong by this
    rule. **[sourced]** ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/hahlatot/punctuation/))
13. **Inflecting vs. transliterating English technical verbs.** Real Israeli
    tech speech freely verbs English nouns into Hebrew binyan patterns —
    **לדבג** (to debug), **לדלוור** (to deliver/ship) — fully inflected Hebrew
    verbs, not "לעשות debug." This code-switched verb-coinage is a well-attested
    feature of Israeli tech speech. **[sourced]** (pattern confirmed across
    Hebrew tech-glossary sources, e.g.
    [infinitylabs.co.il/tech-dictionary](https://infinitylabs.co.il/tech-dictionary/))
    The judgement call for this site's prose register: **"לעשות דיפלוי"**
    reads as a verbal crutch in body copy; **"פרסנו גרסה חדשה"** (we released a
    new version) is cleaner Hebrew for marketing/product copy, while a fully
    inflected slang verb like **לדבג** is fine in a quote or a developer-facing
    aside. **[judgement]**.
14. **Noun number after a numeral.** After the numerals 2–10 the counted noun
    takes the plural (**שישה אחוזים** — six percent, **שלושה שקלים** — three
    shekels); after 11+ the noun may be singular or plural. A generated text
    that mismatches this (singular noun after a 2–10 numeral) is a detectable
    grammar tell, distinct from a style tell. **[sourced]** (search synthesis
    citing [iedit.co.il](https://iedit.co.il/how-to-correctly-write-numbers-in-hebrew/)
    and consistent with Academy numeral-agreement rules)

## 3. Humour at about 10%

**Evidence note up front:** sourcing *specific, attributable* lines of
published Israeli business/tech humour through search returned mostly general
advice ("professional but accessible, with gentle humor and natural Hebrew
expressions is the golden formula for Israeli business communication" —
**[sourced]**, synthesis from
[claudecourse.co.il](https://claudecourse.co.il/blog/eize-ai-hachi-tov-ivrit)
and related guides) rather than quotable, citable sentences from named writers.
The 9 example lines below are therefore **constructed illustrations, marked
[judgement]** — written to demonstrate the technique this research found
described, not verbatim quotes pulled from a real byline. Treat them as a
calibration aid, not as sourced fact.

### What works

1. **"המערכת הזאת לא תחליף אתכם. היא רק תמנע מכם לגלות בבוקר שהיית צריך
   לדעת משהו אתמול."** (This system won't replace you. It'll just stop you
   from finding out in the morning that you needed to know something
   yesterday.) — understatement: downplays the product's value in words while
   the claim underneath is strong. **[judgement]**
2. **"בדקנו את זה על עצמנו קודם, כמו שצריך."** (We tested it on ourselves
   first, like you're supposed to.) — self-deprecation about one's own trade
   (admitting you're the guinea pig) that builds trust rather than spending it.
   **[judgement]**
3. **"זה לא קסם, זה בעיקר אקסל עם שאיפות."** (It's not magic, it's mostly a
   spreadsheet with ambitions.) — a concrete, slightly absurd image instead of
   a joke; deflates the tech-mystique without undercutting the actual claim.
   **[judgement]**
4. **"כל פיצ'ר שביקשתם, חוץ מהקפה. על זה עדיין עובדים."** (Every feature you
   asked for, except the coffee. Still working on that one.) — a deflating
   aside right after a feature list, timed to land after the serious content,
   not instead of it. **[judgement]**
5. **"ניסינו להסביר את זה בלי באזזורדס. זה לקח שתי טיוטות."** (We tried
   explaining this without buzzwords. Took two drafts.) — self-deprecation
   about the writer's own trade (marketing copy), which is the safest target
   for humour in business writing because it doesn't punch at the reader or
   the client. **[judgement]**
6. **"בלי לקטר יותר מהמידה: זה עבד."** (Without complaining more than
   necessary: it worked.) — a dry idiom ("לקטר," to gripe/kvetch) used once,
   sparingly, as connective tissue rather than as the joke itself.
   **[judgement]**
7. **"יש לנו תוצאות. אין לנו עדיין את הביטחון העצמי להגיד 'מהפכה.'"** (We have
   results. We don't yet have the self-confidence to say "revolution.") —
   understatement that directly undercuts the marketing-adjective-stack tell
   from Section 1 (#13, #15), turning the restraint itself into the joke.
   **[judgement]**
8. **"זה עבד בניסיון הראשון. חשדנו משהו."** (It worked on the first try. We
   got suspicious.) — self-deprecating aside about one's own trade (engineers
   distrust easy wins) placed right after a success claim, which keeps the
   claim credible instead of inflating it. **[judgement]**
9. **"קראנו את זה שלוש פעמים כדי לבדוק שאין פה באזזורד מתחזה."** (We read this
   three times to check there's no buzzword hiding in here.) — names the exact
   failure mode (Section 1) as the joke; works because the humour and the
   editorial standard are the same claim. **[judgement]**

### What does not work in Israeli business Hebrew

- **Puns on the brand/client name** — reads as a decision made for the writer's
  amusement, not the reader's; risks looking amateur in client-facing copy.
  **[judgement]**
- **Forced slang** dropped into otherwise formal copy ("זה פשוט וואלה אדיר") —
  the register mismatch is louder than the joke. **[judgement]**
- **Exclamation marks** as a substitute for actual energy in the sentence —
  flagged broadly across Hebrew style guidance as a marker of weak,
  over-eager copy. **[judgement]**
- **Emoji in body text** — fine in a WhatsApp message, reads as unserious in
  a case study or a landing page paragraph. **[judgement]**
- **Jokes at the reader's expense** — "אתם בטח עדיין עושים את זה באקסל" lands
  as condescension, not wit, in Hebrew business writing the same way it would
  in English. **[judgement]**
- **Coy self-reference ("אנחנו לא נשכנע אותך")** — narrates the act of
  persuading instead of persuading; also a direct instance of the English-copy
  calque flagged in Section 1 (#24). **[judgement]**
- **A joke every paragraph** — the brief's own "about 10%" framing matters:
  stacking bits back-to-back reads as a comedy routine, not a business
  document, and erodes the credibility the humour was supposed to be riding on
  top of. **[judgement]**
